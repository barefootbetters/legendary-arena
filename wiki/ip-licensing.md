# IP Licensing

> This page explains the intellectual-property rights Legendary Arena depends on,
> who owns them, what a license realistically costs, and how it would be secured.
> The **authoritative artifact** is the acquisition plan in the marketing-repo
> corporate memory
> (`C:\www\legendary-arena-com\docs\corporate-memory\04-ip-licensing-acquisition-plan.md`)
> and the royalty covenant in [`docs/01-VISION.md`](../docs/01-VISION.md) — this
> page cites both. It is `draft`: some facts below are unverified and marked as
> such.

## Summary

Legendary Arena is a digital *Marvel Legendary* deck-builder, which means it
depends on **two separate intellectual-property rights it does not yet license**:
Upper Deck Entertainment owns the *Legendary* game system and name; Marvel (Disney)
owns the characters and art — and the **digital** character rights are a distinct
grant that Upper Deck never held. The
[Monetization Model](monetization-model.md) and
[`docs/01-VISION.md`](../docs/01-VISION.md) §Financial Sustainability both treat
royalties to Upper Deck and Marvel as a non-negotiable covenant. This page records
the rights that covenant assumes and the realistic shape of securing them.

## Mechanics

### The IP stack — two owners, two separate licenses

| Layer | Owner | License needed |
|-------|-------|----------------|
| *Legendary* game system + brand | **Upper Deck Entertainment** | Upper Deck |
| Characters + art, **digital** rights | **Marvel (The Walt Disney Company)** | Marvel/Disney |

The load-bearing fact: **digital/interactive character rights are negotiated
separately from physical/print rights.** Marvel granted Upper Deck the *physical*
Legendary Marvel license; it did **not** hand UD the digital rights. So an official
digital *Marvel* Legendary is not a single grant — it needs **both** a Marvel
digital license and an Upper Deck system license (or one joint deal that bundles
them). An earlier view that treated Marvel as "downstream" of a single Upper Deck
deal was wrong; the DXP evidence below is why.

### Legendary DXP — the proof it's two rights, and a market gap

Upper Deck already ships a digital Legendary game — **Legendary DXP** — but it uses
an **original fantasy setting (the world of Alamyth), not Marvel.** Developed by
SkyReacher Entertainment, published by Upper Deck; launched 2017, went fully
free-to-play around 2018. It is a mechanical reskin of the Legendary system with
new heroes/villains, and community resources map its names back to Marvel
equivalents.

Why this matters:

- **It proves digital Marvel is a separate grant.** `[CONFIRM]` at the contract
  level, but well-reported: UD built DXP as fantasy *because their Marvel license
  did not cover a digital version.* That establishes digital Marvel rights sit with
  Marvel, not UD.
- **There is no official digital *Marvel* Legendary anywhere.** DXP does not
  compete on the Marvel axis. Legendary Arena would fill a genuine gap the
  rights-holders left empty — the pitch is "grow the pie," not "compete."
- **DXP is a fairness counter-precedent — a selling point.** DXP monetizes with a
  ~$4.99 unlock, paid expansions, a premium **Gold** currency, cosmetics, reward
  chests, and an **energy system** that gates free play. The energy / pay-to-skip
  and randomized-chest levers **diverge from** Legendary Arena's VISION guardrails
  (no energy systems, no pay-to-skip timers, no randomized/mystery goods). "The
  existing digital Legendary gates play behind energy and sells currency; ours is
  fairness-first, zero pay-to-win" is a cleaner brand-steward story for Marvel.

### Deal economics — correcting the "$10k deposit + 10%" assumption

A common assumption is a $10,000 deposit against a 10% royalty. That shape matches
a small tabletop *designer → publisher* royalty deal, **not** a
major-entertainment license — and it ignores that **two licensors may each take a
cut.** Public benchmarks for character/entertainment IP (all `[CONFIRM]` with
counsel and the counterparties):

| Term | Typical range | Note |
|------|---------------|------|
| **Royalty rate** | **15–30%** of net revenue **per licensor** | Leaked Sony–Marvel terms imply ~19–26% on a digital title; 10% is low for Marvel-branded |
| **Two licensors** | Marvel *and* Upper Deck may each take a royalty | The cumulative load is the real number |
| **Advance** | ~50% of first-year minimum guarantee | A ~$10k *advance* is plausible only if the guarantee is small |
| **Minimum guarantee (MG)** | 25–50% of projected royalties; **non-refundable** | The MG — not the advance — is the real floor commitment |
| **Approval rights** | Licensor approves creative, marketing, use of marks | Marvel especially wants presentation approval |

The reframe: **$10k is at most an advance line item for one licensor, not the
price of the arrangement.** For anything carrying the Marvel brand the true
commitment — `(MG + rate) × up to two licensors + legal/agent` — is materially
larger. Disney/Marvel grant major franchises to well-capitalized, established
partners over a **6–18 month** timeline.

### How it would be secured (shape, not a runbook)

The plan's recommended sequence for an independent/small team: **(1)** prove the
product with a **license-clean, non-Marvel build** and real player metrics — no
Marvel IP in anything shown. That build can be skinned with **original**,
**fantasy** (DXP-style), or **verified public-domain Golden Age hero** content;
the PD-superhero route keeps the superhero genre (a more on-brand UD pitch) while
needing only the Upper Deck system license, not Marvel — but `[CONFIRM]` PD status
is a per-character *and per-issue* legal question (some titles were renewed; later
publishers made copyrighted versions of otherwise-PD heroes), so counsel must
clear each one. Then **(2)** approach **Upper Deck** with a
partnership/royalty proposal (UD routinely puts new IPs on the Legendary system —
Alien, Predator, Big Trouble in Little China, Firefly, Buffy — and already
partnered a studio for digital via DXP, so this is an on-pattern ask); **(3)** go
to Marvel **jointly**, UD as broker, ideally a tri-party deal. A high-probability
alternative to going solo is **partnering with an established digital board-game
studio** (Dire Wolf Digital, Asmodee Digital) that already holds IP-holder
relationships — trading equity/control for credibility and open doors. Retain IP
counsel before any outreach. The step-by-step execution lives in the plan doc;
this page records the strategy, not the checklist.

## Edge Cases

- **Two licenses, not one.** Marvel (digital character rights) *and* Upper Deck
  (Legendary system/brand) are both required; a single deal with either is
  insufficient for an official digital Marvel Legendary.
- **Upper Deck may not be able to deliver Marvel.** The digital Marvel license is a
  separate Disney negotiation. `[CONFIRM]` UD's own Marvel relationship is in flux
  — it lost the Marvel *trading card* license to Topps (effective 2025); if the
  *physical* Marvel Legendary license has also lapsed, UD is a weaker broker and
  weight shifts to a direct Marvel approach.
- **DXP is fantasy, not Marvel — not a competitor.** The overlap risk is on the
  *system* (Upper Deck's), not on Marvel; there is no official digital Marvel
  Legendary to compete with. This is a market gap, and DXP's energy/currency model
  is a fairness contrast Legendary Arena can pitch against.
- **"$10k / 10%" is a misconception to correct, not a plan input.** With two
  possible royalty stacks, it under-budgets a Marvel-branded license by a wide
  margin.
- **The currently-public build shows Marvel IP.** The plan's "show a license-clean
  build" guidance is not just about the demo — legendary-arena.com is live today
  with actual Marvel names and art, so it's a decision about the already-public
  product: reskin the public build to fantasy (DXP-style) and keep Marvel private
  for the licensed release, or accept the standing exposure. Counsel should weigh in
  before any outreach (outreach points the rights-holders at whatever is public).
- **This is not a decision.** No license has been pursued or granted; the plan is
  pre-outreach. When it moves to commitment, that lands in the marketing-repo
  decision log, and this page's status updates then.
